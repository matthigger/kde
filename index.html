KDE, visually

Build a pdf from the samples themselves: put a bump on every sample and average them. No shape assumed; one knob, the bandwidth h.

An average of n bumps

Goal: estimate the pdf that samples x1, ..., xn came from, without assuming its shape (Gaussian, exponential, ...).

Approach: (Rosenblatt 1956; Parzen 1962) center a kernel K, any pdf centered at 0, on every sample, stretch it by the bandwidth h, and average.

p̂(x) = 1n ∑i Kh(x − xi),   Kh(u) = 1h K(u/h)

Dividing by h keeps each bump's area at 1, so the average of n bumps has area 1 too: p̂ is a pdf. With the Gaussian K, Kh(x − xi) is the N(xi, h2) pdf: h is each bump's standard deviation.

Bins vs. bumps

Goal: the same estimate from many samples of a pdf we know (dashed), so each estimate can be checked against the truth.

Approach: a histogram counts the samples in x's bin, with bins fixed in advance; a KDE averages a bump on each sample, so nothing is fixed but h.

histogram: p̂(x) = # samples in x's binn w vs. KDE: p̂(x) = 1n ∑i Kh(x − xi)

w is the bin width. "Distance from the truth" is ½∫|p̂ − p| dx: 0 for a perfect estimate, 1 when the two share no area. In practice the truth is unknown, which is what the next tab is about.

Choosing h: held-out log-likelihood

Goal: pick the h whose p̂ makes data it never saw most probable, using only the samples.

Approach: k-fold cross-validation. Build p̂−j from every fold but j, score fold j by its mean ln p̂−j, average over folds, and keep the h with the highest score.

score(h) = 1k ∑j meanxi ∈ fold j ln p̂−j(xi)

Scott's rule h = σn−1/5 (Scott 1992), with σ the samples' standard deviation, is a quick first guess built for Gaussian-shaped data; it is where the tab starts. The green curve scores p̂ on new data from the truth, ∫p ln p̂, which only a known truth allows.

Try this

  • Slide h from 0.05 up to 5. How many peaks does p̂ have at each end?
    Answer On six points with the Gaussian kernel: six spikes, one per sample, until h ≈ 0.2; one wide bump from h ≈ 1 on. In between, the samples that sit close together merge first: at h = 0.6 the four on the left are one hump and the two on the right another.
  • Watch area under p̂ while you drag samples and change h or the kernel. Why does it never move?
    AnswerEach bump Kh(x − xi) is a pdf, area 1: shrinking h makes it narrower and taller by the same factor. Each enters with weight 1/n, and n areas of 1/n add to 1.
  • Pick tophat. What shape is p̂, and where does it jump?
    AnswerA staircase. Each sample adds a box of width 2h and height 1/(2nh), so p̂(x) = (samples within h of x) / (2nh), and it jumps at every xi ± h. It is a histogram whose bin of width 2h is centered on x instead of fixed in advance.
  • Press Draw one a few times, then Draw 100. How is each draw made, and where do the draws pile up?
    AnswerPick a sample at random (chance 1/n each), then add h times a draw from K: the new point lands under that sample's bump. That is exactly a draw from p̂, so the histogram of many draws traces p̂.
  • On two humps, drag the bin count from 2 up to 200 and watch the histogram's distance from the truth.
    Answer Too few bins blur the humps into blocks (2 bins: about 0.4); too many give a jagged skyline of empty and overfull bins, "gap teeth" (200 bins: about 0.16). The best is in between, around 25 to 30 bins (about 0.07). The KDE beats it: about 0.05 for h near 0.2 to 0.3.
  • Set about 10 bins and drag shift. What changes, and what does the KDE have in its place?
    AnswerThe samples stay put, yet the histogram's shape changes: where the edges fall is a second, arbitrary choice. The KDE has no edges to place; each bump sits on its own sample.
  • Press tophat, h = w/2, then switch the kernel to Gaussian at the same h.
    Answer The tophat KDE is a histogram whose bin, of the same width w = 2h, slides along centered on x: the same blockiness, but no edges to place, and already a bit closer to the truth. The Gaussian kernel at the same h smooths away the steps and gets closer still.
  • Pick flat, hard edges. What does every kernel do at ±2?
    AnswerEach sample near an edge spills part of its bump past it, so p̂ ramps down over a few h around the edge instead of dropping straight to 0. A smaller h narrows the ramp but makes the flat top noisier.
  • Drag h all the way left on the chart. Why does the training score climb forever while the held-out score falls?
    AnswerEach training sample sits under its own bump, of height K(0)/(nh), which grows without bound as h → 0: the training score rewards memorizing the samples. A held-out sample has no bump of its own, so as the bumps shrink onto the training samples, p̂ at the held-out samples falls toward 0.
  • Compare Scott's rule with the CV best on two humps, then on one bell.
    Answer On one bell they roughly agree (both near 0.3): Scott's rule is built for Gaussian-shaped data. On two humps Scott's σ counts the gap between the humps as spread, so it gives about 0.85 and flattens the humps, while CV picks about 0.4. Check the green curve: CV's pick sits near the new-data peak.
  • Pick tophat and fold 1, then slide h down. Where does the held-out curve end, and why?
    AnswerOnce some held-out sample has no training sample within h, its box misses every bump: p̂ = 0 there (a red dot), ln 0 = −∞, and so is the fold's mean. With all samples, the 5-fold curve starts near 0.7 on two humps: a single lonely tail sample is enough to zero its fold, so CV ends up at the smallest h that reaches every sample. The Gaussian kernel is never exactly 0, so its curve falls steeply but never ends.
  • Pick sharp peak, wide base and press h = CV best. How well does one h serve both parts?
    AnswerNot well. The peak (standard deviation 0.15) wants h well under 0.15, the thin tails a big h. Depending on the draw, CV blunts the peak (h near 0.4) or leaves wiggles in the tails (h near 0.15); press New samples to see both. One h for the whole line is the method's limit.
